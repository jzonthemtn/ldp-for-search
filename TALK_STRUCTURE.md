## The abstract this has to deliver

> To build a good search experience, you need to know exactly what your users are doing. The
> OpenSearch User Behavior Insights (UBI) framework exists for this, but what happens when the
> legal department says you can't store the queries? In high-stakes industries like healthcare
> and finance, zero-trust is no longer just a buzzword because now it is a blocker to relevance
> tuning.
>
> This session demonstrates using Local Differential Privacy (LDP) to privatize user search
> queries and interaction logs directly on the client's device before they reach an OpenSearch
> cluster. By injecting noise at the source, we can capture the behavioral trends needed for
> learning-to-rank (LTR) and query-intent analysis without ever seeing the raw PII. Instead of
> just hoping the data is actually private, we demonstrate how to verify the injected noise.
>
> Attendees will learn how to balance privacy and search utility and move away from reactive
> data scrubbing using OpenSearch and UBI.

## Three promises to keep

The abstract makes three specific commitments. Each one needs a moment on stage that discharges
it, or the talk under-delivers.

1. **Privatize on the client, before the cluster.** Notebook sections 3 and 5.
2. **Keep the trends needed for LTR and query-intent analysis.** Notebook section 11. This is
   the promise most at risk, because everything else in the notebook measures what LDP costs.
3. **Verify, do not hope.** Notebook sections 8 and 10.

## Running order

Timings assume a 45 minute slot with 5 for questions. The 20 minute cut is noted at the end.

### Part 1. The blocker (slides, 8 min)

UBI and what it captures. The `ubi_queries` index with `user_query`, `query_id`, `client_id`.
The `ubi_events` index with `action_name`, `session_id`, `position`, `object_id`. This is the
raw material relevance tuning runs on.

Then name the problem precisely. Legal does not object to "search data" in the abstract. They
object to `user_query`, which is free text a user typed and may contain anything, and to
`client_id` plus `session_id`, which make it linkable across time.

Close the act on why scrubbing is the wrong shape of solution. You collected the PII first, then
tried to remove it, and you only discover a failure after the fact. The alternative is that the
sensitive value never leaves the device in readable form.

### Part 2. Embeddings are not anonymization (notebook, 5 min)

Run sections 1 through 6, then section 9.

This exists to kill the objection half the room is already forming, which is "we will just store
the embedding instead of the text." Section 9 shows the raw query vector inverting cleanly back
to `Laptop`. An embedding is a lossy encoding, not a privacy control.

Read the 20 documents aloud. They fit in one breath and the audience can hold the whole index in
their head, which makes the next result land without any metric.

### Part 4. What it costs, honestly (notebook, 7 min)

Section 7 on the toy index. The real index was already attacked in Part 3.

Volunteer the weakness here rather than letting it come out in questions. At `epsilon = 1.2`

The real-index finding, that the two curves separate around `epsilon = 10` to `20`, now lives in

Plot: `plots/07_epsilon_tradeoff_toy_index.png`

### Part 5. What you keep (notebook, 8 min)

Section 11. This is the payoff and the answer to Part 4.

The noise is zero-mean, so it cancels across users. One person's query is unrecoverable. The
trend across a cohort is exact. At `epsilon = 1.0`, which is heavier noise than anything in Part
4, all five cohorts are identified correctly from noised data alone, while individual recovery
sits at roughly chance.

The convergence plot is the centrepiece. Error falls as `1 / sqrt(n)`, matching theory, and
crosses below the spread of the index itself at a few thousand users per segment. That number is
the practical takeaway: this works if your segments have thousands of users, and it does not if
they have dozens.

Frame the constraint correctly. LDP does not cost you analytics. It costs you the ability to ask
about one person. Every input relevance tuning actually needs is a population statistic.

Plot: `plots/11_aggregate_convergence.png`

### Part 6. Back to OpenSearch, and the limits (slides, 7 min)

Where injection sits in `ubi.js`, on the device, before the event is sent.

What your dashboards can still compute, and what they cannot. Then state the limits plainly,
because each of these is a question someone will ask anyway.

- The category leaks. Section 10 measured it. For furniture that is harmless. For a medical or
  legal corpus it may not be, and this mechanism at this epsilon would not be sufficient.
- Epsilon does not transfer between indexes. The useful range was around 1.2 on the toy index
  and 10 to 50 on the 43k index, because what matters is the noise scale relative to the spread
  of the vector coordinates.
- Low-traffic segments stay unmeasurable. The tail of the query distribution never reaches the
  population sizes Part 5 depends on.
- Section 11 covers query vectors. Interaction events such as clicks are counts rather than
  vectors, and want a different mechanism, typically randomized response.

## Numbers worth having memorized

Setup: `all-MiniLM-L6-v2`, 384 dimensions reduced to 20 by PCA, retaining 41.9% of variance.
Real index is WANDS, 42,994 Wayfair products, 861 product classes, MIT licensed.

**Toy index, 20 documents, section 7.** Random chance is P@1 0.05 and R@5 0.25.

| epsilon | P@1 | R@5 |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 1.2 | 0.07 | 0.45 |
| 3 | 0.38 | 0.75 |
| 5 | 0.72 | 0.95 |

**Real index, 42,994 products, section 10.** Chance is item 0.00002 and class 0.026.

| epsilon | item P@1 | class P@1 |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 5 | 0.04 | 0.33 |
| 10 | 0.13 | 0.63 |
| 20 | 0.42 | 0.82 |
| 50 | 0.93 | 0.98 |

**Aggregates, section 11.** At `epsilon = 1.0` with 20,000 users per cohort, 5 of 5 cohorts
identified correctly. Individual recovery runs about 1x to 2.5x chance at the category level and
is never usable at the item level.

Centroid error against population size, cohort `Beds`:

| users | error |
|---:|---:|
| 10 | 2.098 |
| 100 | 0.638 |
| 1,000 | 0.194 |
| 10,000 | 0.066 |
| 100,000 | 0.020 |

The spread of the index itself is 0.128, which the error crosses at roughly 2,500 users.

That crossing is at `epsilon = 1.0` on this index, not a universal number. Error goes as
`(1 / epsilon) / sqrt(n)`, so the threshold scales as `1 / epsilon^2`: around 10,000 users at
`epsilon = 0.5`, around 625 at `epsilon = 2`. Slide 25 states this as "halving epsilon needs four
times the users".

## Questions to expect

**"Is epsilon = 1.0 in section 11 comparable to epsilon = 10 in section 10?"** Yes, same index
and same PCA basis. Section 11 uses far heavier noise and still recovers the aggregate, which is
the point.

**"Why PCA at all?"** It concentrates the signal so the noise budget is spread over 20
coordinates instead of 384. Note honestly that it retains only 41.9% of variance, and that the
PCA basis itself is fitted on the corpus, not on user queries.

**"What is the actual privacy guarantee?"** Be careful here. The notebook demonstrates the
mechanism and audits it empirically. A formal epsilon-DP claim requires the sensitivity to be
calibrated to the true coordinate range, and the notebook uses `sensitivity = 1.0` throughout.
Say that plainly if pressed.

**"Does this compose across many queries from one user?"** No, and this is the sharpest
limitation. Privacy budget accumulates across repeated queries from the same client. Per-query
epsilon is not per-user epsilon.

**"Why not just hash the query?"** Hashing is reversible by dictionary attack over a query
vocabulary, and it destroys the semantic structure that makes aggregation useful.
