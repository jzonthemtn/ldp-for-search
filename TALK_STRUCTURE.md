# Talk structure

**Leveraging LDP for High-Trust OpenSearch UBI**
OpenSearchCon NA. September 24, 2026. Jeff Zemerick.

Working plan for the session. Slides carry the OpenSearch and UBI story, the notebook
carries the search and privacy theory. The deck is generated by `build_slides.py`, so
edit the `SLIDES` list there rather than the .pptx. For the mechanics of running the
notebook on stage, see [RUNNING_THE_DEMO.md](RUNNING_THE_DEMO.md).

## The abstract this has to deliver

> To build a good search experience, you need to know exactly what your users are doing. The
> OpenSearch User Behavior Insights (UBI) framework exists for this, but what happens when the
> legal department says you can't store the queries? In high-stakes industries like healthcare
> and finance, zero-trust is no longer just a buzzword because now it is a blocker to relevance
> tuning.
>
> This session demonstrates using Local Differential Privacy (LDP) to privatize user search
> queries and interaction logs directly on the client's device before they reach an OpenSearch
> cluster. By injecting noise at the source, we can capture the behavioral trends needed for
> learning-to-rank (LTR) and query-intent analysis without ever seeing the raw PII. Instead of
> just hoping the data is actually private, we demonstrate how to verify the injected noise.
>
> Attendees will learn how to balance privacy and search utility and move away from reactive
> data scrubbing using OpenSearch and UBI.

## Three promises to keep

Each commitment needs a moment on stage that discharges it, or the talk under-delivers.

1. **Privatize on the client, before the cluster.** Notebook sections 3 and 5.
2. **Keep the trends needed for LTR and query-intent analysis.** Notebook section 11. Most
   at risk, because everything else in the notebook measures what LDP costs.
3. **Verify, do not hope.** Notebook sections 8 and 10.

## Running order

45 minute slot with 5 for questions. The 20 minute cut is at the end.

### Part 1. The problem (slides, 8 min)

UBI and what it captures: `ubi_queries` with `user_query`, `query_id`, `client_id`, and
`ubi_events` with `action_name`, `session_id`, `position`, `object_id`. This is the raw
material relevance tuning runs on.

Then name the problem precisely. Legal does not object to "search data" in the abstract.
They object to `user_query`, free text that may contain anything, and to `client_id` plus
`session_id`, which make it linkable across time.

Close on why scrubbing is the wrong shape of solution: you collected the PII first, and you
only discover a failure after the fact. The alternative is that the sensitive value never
leaves the device readable.

### Part 2. Embeddings are not anonymization (slides, 5 min)

This kills the objection half the room is already forming, "we will just store the embedding
instead of the text". Section 9's result is on a slide: the raw query vector inverts cleanly
back to `Laptop`, while the noised one lands 4.9 away on `Gaming Console`. An embedding is a
lossy encoding, not a privacy control.

Read the 20 documents aloud. They fit in one breath, so the audience holds the whole index in
their head and the result lands without any metric.

### Part 3. The fix and the proof (slides plus notebook, 7 min)

One slide: Laplace noise, scale is `sensitivity / epsilon`, epsilon is the dial.

Then two verification beats, weakest to strongest.

- Section 8, the tent histogram. The mechanism is distributionally what it claims.
  `plots/08_laplace_tent_audit.png`
- Section 10, the attack curve. An actual adversary failing, measured. Stronger evidence,
  so give it more time. `plots/10_item_vs_class_recovery.png`

This discharges "instead of just hoping the data is actually private".

### Part 4. What it costs (notebook, 7 min)

Section 7, on the toy index. The real index was already attacked in Part 3.

Volunteer the weakness rather than letting it surface in questions. The section 9 demo was
rigged at `epsilon = 1.2`: privacy excellent, utility destroyed, barely above random. Saying
so yourself is what buys credibility for Part 5.

The real-index finding, that the curves separate around `epsilon = 10` to `20`, now lives in
Part 3. Refer back in a sentence if needed, but do not re-show the plot.

`plots/07_epsilon_tradeoff_toy_index.png`

### Part 5. What you keep (notebook, 8 min)

Section 11. The payoff, and the answer to Part 4.

The noise is zero-mean, so it cancels across users. One person's query is unrecoverable, the
trend across a cohort is exact. At `epsilon = 1.0`, heavier noise than anything in Part 4, all
five cohorts are identified correctly from noised data alone while individual recovery sits at
roughly chance.

The convergence plot is the centrepiece. Error falls as `1 / sqrt(n)`, matching theory, and
crosses the spread of the index at a few thousand users. That is the practical takeaway: this
works if your segments have thousands of users, not if they have dozens.

Frame the constraint correctly. LDP does not cost you analytics, it costs you the ability to
ask about one person. Every input relevance tuning needs is a population statistic.

`plots/11_aggregate_convergence.png`

### Part 6. Back in OpenSearch (slides, 7 min)

Where injection sits in `ubi.js`: on the device, before the event is sent. What dashboards can
still compute, and what they cannot. Then the limits, plainly, because each is a question
someone will ask anyway.

- The category leaks. Harmless for furniture, possibly not for a medical or legal corpus.
- What a given epsilon buys you does not transfer between indexes, because what matters is the
  noise scale relative to the spread of the coordinates. The toy index and the 43k index are
  not comparable on this axis.
- Low-traffic segments stay unmeasurable. The tail never reaches the population sizes Part 5
  depends on.
- Section 11 covers query vectors. Clicks are counts rather than vectors and want a different
  mechanism, typically randomized response.

## Numbers worth having memorized

Setup: `all-MiniLM-L6-v2`, 384 dimensions reduced to 20 by PCA, retaining 41.9% of variance.
Real index is WANDS, 42,994 Wayfair products, 861 product classes, MIT licensed.

**Toy index, 20 documents, section 7.** Chance is P@1 0.05 and R@5 0.25.

| epsilon | P@1 | R@5 |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 1.2 | 0.07 | 0.45 |
| 3 | 0.38 | 0.75 |
| 5 | 0.72 | 0.95 |

**Real index, 42,994 products, section 10.** Chance is item 0.00002 and class 0.026.

| epsilon | item P@1 | class P@1 |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 5 | 0.04 | 0.33 |
| 10 | 0.13 | 0.63 |
| 20 | 0.42 | 0.82 |
| 50 | 0.93 | 0.98 |

**Aggregates, section 11.** At `epsilon = 1.0` with 20,000 users per cohort, 5 of 5 cohorts
identified correctly. Individual recovery runs about 1x to 2.5x chance at the category level
and is never usable at the item level.

Centroid error against population size, cohort `Beds`:

| users | error |
|---:|---:|
| 10 | 2.098 |
| 100 | 0.638 |
| 1,000 | 0.194 |
| 10,000 | 0.066 |
| 100,000 | 0.020 |

The spread of the index is 0.128, which the error crosses at roughly 2,300 users.

That crossing is at `epsilon = 1.0` on this index, not a universal number. Error goes as
`(1 / epsilon) / sqrt(n)`, so the threshold scales as `1 / epsilon^2`: around 9,200 users at
`epsilon = 0.5`, around 575 at `epsilon = 2`. Slide 23 states this as "halving epsilon needs
four times the users".

## Questions to expect

**"Is epsilon = 1.0 in section 11 comparable to epsilon = 10 in section 10?"** Yes, same index
and same PCA basis. Section 11 uses far heavier noise and still recovers the aggregate, which
is the point.

**"Why PCA at all?"** It concentrates the signal so the noise budget covers 20 coordinates
instead of 384. Note honestly that it retains only 41.9% of variance, and that the basis is
fitted on the corpus, not on user queries.

**"What is the actual privacy guarantee?"** Be careful. The notebook demonstrates the mechanism
and audits it empirically. A formal epsilon-DP claim requires sensitivity calibrated to the
true coordinate range, and the notebook uses `sensitivity = 1.0` throughout. Say that plainly
if pressed.

**"What happens across many queries from one user?"** The budget composes, and this is the
sharpest limitation. Per-query epsilon is not per-user epsilon, and an attacker who averages
one user's repeated noised draws recovers the intent. The fix is rotating or dropping
`client_id` and `session_id`, not a smaller epsilon.

**"How often does epsilon need re-tuning?"** When you re-embed or refit PCA. Routine additions
to a large index do not move the coordinate spread.

**"Why not just hash the query?"** Hashing is reversible by dictionary attack over a query
vocabulary, and it destroys the semantic structure that makes aggregation useful.

## Cutting to 20 minutes

Keep Parts 1, 2 and 5. Compress Part 3 to the attack curve alone and drop the tent histogram to
a slide. Drop Part 4 entirely and state the tradeoff in one sentence over the section 10 plot.
Keep the limits from Part 6, shortened to a single slide.

The three that cannot go are the problem, the proof that embeddings leak, and the proof that
aggregates survive.
